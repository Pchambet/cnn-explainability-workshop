Does an eye bar stop a CNN from recognising a face?

Not this one. With one clean photo per person, an off-the-shelf ImageNet VGG16 still re-identifies 13.9% of eye-barred faces among 62 people (17.5% without the bar, chance 1.6%). Explainability methods show why: the matcher spreads its evidence well beyond the eyes.

The question

A black bar across the eyes is the oldest way to "anonymise" a face in a photo. The CNIL's innovation lab notes that it "may be ineffective against a facial recognition algorithm, which could find enough markers on the face to identify the person" (LINC, 2023). This report measures that claim for a generic CNN: if someone holds one ordinary photo of each person, can they still name the person behind the bar?

Protocol

  1. Data. Labeled Faces in the Wild (deep-funneled, aligned): the 62 people with at least 20 photos, first 20 photos each (1,240 images). Crop includes hair, ears and collar.
  2. Attack. One clean photo per person is enrolled; each of the other 1,178 photos is masked and matched to the closest enrolled photo by cosine similarity. Repeated over 20 random enrolment draws, the same draws for every mask (paired comparison).
  3. Matchers. VGG16 pool5 features, flattened (25,088-d, the design of the first version of this workshop) or averaged (512-d); eigenfaces fitted on 96 other LFW identities (1,301 images); raw pixels.
  4. Masks. Eye bar, eyes-and-nose bar, mild and strong Gaussian blur of the face, face blacked out. Masks are fixed boxes on the aligned crop.

The flattened VGG16 matcher keeps 13.9% of 17.5% under the eye bar, 8.6 times chance. A wider bar over the eyes and nose leaves 13.1%, mild blur 13.8%, strong blur 12.8% and a blacked-out face 10.8%: what remains comes from hair, face outline, collar and the photo itself. Keeping the spatial layout (flattened) makes the matcher markedly more robust to blur than averaging it away. Eigenfaces start lower (11.6%) and fall to 6.3% with the bar.

A free counter-move: bar the enrolment photo too

The bar creates a mismatch between a masked probe and an unmasked reference. An attacker can remove that mismatch by drawing the same bar on the reference. Then the flattened matcher re-identifies 17.2% of barred probes and the averaged one 18.4%: back at, or above, their unmasked accuracy.

What the bar does break: a fixed threshold

Threshold calibrated on unmasked photos at a 1% false-accept rate, then frozen.

A verifier that says "same person" above a threshold accepts only 5% of genuine pairs at 1% false accepts even without a mask (this is not a face model), and 0.1% with the bar: the bar lowers every similarity score, impostors included, so a frozen threshold rejects nearly everything. That is a calibration effect, not anonymity: ranking barely moves (AUC 0.69 → 0.67) and an attacker simply recalibrates. Unmasked, eigenfaces are the better verifier at this operating point (10.1%) even though the CNN is the better identifier. The first version's fixed threshold of 0.5 accepted 26% of genuine pairs and 10.6% of impostors.

Why: where the matcher looks

Hiding a 40-pixel patch of the probe and measuring the drop in similarity to the enrolled photo, averaged over the 62 aligned identities, gives the matcher's sensitivity map. The eye band holds 12% of it for 10% of the image area, roughly its fair share; the peak sits at mouth level. An eye bar hides the part of the face this matcher needs least.

What an ImageNet CNN sees in a portrait

VGG16 knows 1,000 ImageNet classes and no identities, so on a portrait it predicts jersey (p = 0.32); Grad-CAM at block5 and occlusion both peak on the neck and clothing. Occlusion is not monotone here: hiding most patches slightly raises p(jersey), a reminder that a single-image map is an illustration, not evidence. The first version of this workshop masked this portrait and reported the change in that probability. That measures a clothing classifier, not re-identification, which is why the LFW experiment above replaces it. For the record:

Limitations

  • The matcher is a generic ImageNet network, not a face recogniser. A dedicated model (e.g. ArcFace) is far stronger; whether it relies more or less on the eyes was not tested here.
  • 62 identities of public figures, mostly news photos; many photos of one person come from the same event, which helps a matcher through background and clothing. That is realistic for an attacker but inflates what "identity" means.
  • Masks are fixed boxes on aligned faces; real eye bars are drawn per photo and may be tighter or looser.
  • Intervals reflect enrolment-photo variability only, not the sampling of identities.
  • Grad-CAM and occlusion on the portrait are single-image illustrations, not evidence.
